Read this first. No one can reliably forecast which sector will win next. This page shows two honest things instead: (1) which sectors currently have the strongest evidence-based signals (momentum and trend, which research has found to persist modestly for months, not years), and (2) a test of how well past winners actually went on to win in this data. Use it as one input, never as a tip.

Strategy backtests: what would have worked?

Top-3 momentum: each month hold the 3 sectors with the best 12-1 month return. Min-variance: each January, the lowest-risk sector mix from the prior 5 years. All-sector EW: equal weight in every sector. No costs or taxes included.

How the sector portfolios were built

US (Feb 1990 – Oct 2026): 21 Fidelity Select sector mutual funds, each a professionally run portfolio of the stocks in one industry (banks, chemicals, semiconductors, etc.), using total-return prices (dividends reinvested, after fund fees). Fertilizers have no long-running US fund, so "Agri & Fertilizers" is an equal-weight basket of Mosaic, CF Industries, Nutrien, ADM and Deere. Benchmark: S&P 500 Total Return. Risk-free rate: 13-week US Treasury bill.

India (Aug 1997 – Oct 2026): 15 sectors, each an equal-weight, monthly-rebalanced basket of 3–8 established NSE stocks (85 stocks in total), including dedicated Chemicals and Fertilizers baskets. Stocks enter when their price history begins (mostly 1996–2007). Benchmark: BSE Sensex. Risk-free rate: approximate annual 91-day T-bill yields (RBI). Price returns, so dividends (roughly 1–1.5% a year) are excluded on both sides.

Data: monthly prices from Yahoo Finance, pulled on 1 Nov 2026; the latest bar is October 2026. Full-period comparisons start when each sector's data starts (see the Since column).

Cleaning: 190 Indian stock-months were excluded as bad data, mostly spike-and-reverse errors from mishandled stock splits (e.g. a +1,300% month undone the next month) plus each stock's first 2 months. They're listed below.

What each measure means

CAGR
Compound annual growth rate: the steady yearly return that would turn the starting value into the ending value.
Volatility
Annualised standard deviation of monthly returns. The typical size of swings.
Sharpe ratio
(Return − risk-free rate) ÷ volatility. Reward per unit of total risk. Above ~0.5 over decades is good for a single sector.
Sortino ratio
Like Sharpe, but only counts downside volatility, so upside bursts aren't penalised.
Treynor ratio
(Return − risk-free) ÷ beta. Reward per unit of market risk; useful when the sector is one piece of a diversified portfolio.
Beta
Sensitivity to the benchmark. 1.2 = moves about 1.2% for each 1% market move.
Jensen's alpha
Annual return beyond what beta alone would predict.
Information ratio
Average return over the benchmark ÷ tracking error (volatility of that difference). Consistency of outperformance.
Max drawdown
The worst peak-to-trough fall.
Calmar ratio
CAGR ÷ |max drawdown|. Growth per unit of worst-case pain.
Up / down capture
Average sector return in the market's up (down) months as a % of the market's. Ideal: high up, low down.
VaR 95%
A bad month: returns were worse than this only 5% of the time.

Caveats worth knowing

Survivorship bias (India): baskets are made of companies that exist and are well known today, which flatters past returns, by roughly 3–8 points a year against official NSE indices since 2011 (see India reality check). Rankings are more trustworthy than levels.

Fund proxies (US): Fidelity Select funds are actively managed and charge ~0.7% a year, so they trail a pure index slightly and may tilt within their sector.

Currencies: US numbers are in USD, India in INR. They aren't directly comparable: the rupee lost value against the dollar over this period, and Indian cash rates were higher, which is why Indian Sharpe ratios look lower than their returns suggest.

Not advice: this is historical analysis for learning and research, not a recommendation to buy or sell anything.
